Black skin cancer (melanoma)

Early detection and assessment by a dermatologist in Zurich

Detected early, melanoma can be treated well. Self-checks and medical examinations work hand in hand.

At Dermetrics, the practice of Dr. med. Paul J. Georg, specialist in Dermatology and Venereology and Expert in dermatologic surgery, at Clinic Selnau in Zurich Enge (district 2, Selnau), we examine the skin for skin cancer and remove suspicious lesions. All services

Responsible: Dr. med. Paul J. Georg, specialist in Dermatology and Venereology · Updated: 15 November 2026

In short: Melanoma (black skin cancer) is a malignant disease of the pigment cells. In Switzerland, a good 3,500 people are newly diagnosed with it every year. Detected early, the outlook is very good: the relative five-year survival rate is over 95 out of 100. Monthly self-checks and a dermatological examination based on your risk are important.

Overview

Melanoma develops from melanocytes, the cells that produce the dark skin pigment melanin. Unlike the much more common white skin cancer, it can form secondary tumours (metastases) in lymph nodes or organs even when small. More than two thirds of all melanomas arise on previously normal skin, not from existing moles.

What matters is how deeply the tumour has penetrated the skin when it is discovered. In the precursor stage (in situ) it lies only in the top layer of the skin. Complete surgical removal is then sufficient, and the survival rate is 99 to 100 out of 100. If the cancer has already spread to distant organs, the outlook falls considerably. Modern immunotherapies have improved it, but treatment is then often demanding and lengthy.

Switzerland is one of the countries with the highest skin cancer rates. Over the past 35 years, the annual incidence rate has more than doubled. The main cause is UV radiation, for example during sport and leisure in the mountains or by the water. Thanks to earlier diagnoses and better therapies, mortality has remained stable or fallen slightly.

Early detection

Monthly self-check

Look at your skin once a month in good light, with a mirror or the help of someone close to you. This is how you notice real changes. We explain the ABCDE rule on the page Skin self-examination.

Full-body examination by a specialist

Physical examination of the entire skin by a specialist in dermatology is considered the gold standard of early detection. Suspicious lesions are examined more closely with a dermatoscope, an illuminated special magnifier.

Total body photography with Deviskan

Deviskan by the Spanish manufacturer Dermavision Solutions combines total body photography in 2D and 3D (total body photography, total body mapping or mole mapping) with automated digital dermoscopy. The procedure is comparable to FotoFinder or Vectra by Canfield. With the help of artificial intelligence, almost all visible moles are detected, assigned to body sites and compared with earlier images at follow-up examinations. The imaging takes around 15 minutes. The medical assessment is always made by the doctor.

A short, anonymous questionnaire on dermetrics.ch gives an initial assessment of your skin cancer risk. Go to the pre-check

When you should not wait for the next check-up

  • A mole or skin lesion bleeds, weeps, crusts or does not heal like a wound
  • A spot newly itches, burns or hurts
  • A dark streak under a fingernail or toenail
  • A new or changed dark spot on the palms or soles of the feet
  • When checking yourself, also remember the scalp, ears, lips, inside of the mouth, nails, spaces between the toes, skin folds and the genital area.

Who has an increased risk

  • Melanoma in parents, biological siblings or your own children
  • Previous black or white skin cancer yourself, or precursors (actinic keratoses)
  • More than 50 moles or several unusually shaped (atypical) moles
  • Very fair, sun-sensitive skin, freckles, red or light blond hair
  • Severe sunburns, especially in childhood, a lot of time outdoors, or sunbed use before the age of 35
  • An immune system weakened by medication or illness, for example after an organ transplant

Melanoma in the family

About 8 out of 100 people with a new melanoma have an affected first-degree relative. With a hereditary predisposition, melanoma occurs on average around 20 years earlier. In about 4 out of 10 known melanoma families, certain genetic changes can be detected that can greatly increase lifetime risk. A 2025 study shows that members of «possible» melanoma families also develop the disease similarly often and should be checked just as consistently.

General screening for people without risk factors is debated, because a clear benefit at average risk has not been reliably demonstrated. People at increased risk, on the other hand, benefit from regular check-ups tailored to their profile, because melanomas are then found at earlier, thinner stages.

How the examination works

  • A short conversation about your history, conspicuous lesions and skin cancer in the family
  • Examination of the entire skin from the scalp to the soles of the feet, including nails, mucous membranes and skin folds
  • Examination of conspicuous lesions with the dermatoscope. The examination is painless.
  • Please come without nail polish, make-up, piercings and earrings if possible, and with a simple hairstyle
  • Suspicious lesions are usually removed promptly under local anaesthesia and examined histologically in the laboratory
  • The interval until the next check-up depends on your personal risk.

Limitations

  • Full-body images and computer or AI analyses complement the medical examination but do not replace it. The scalp, soles of the feet, skin folds and genital area must be checked by hand.
  • The pre-check is a statistical guide, not a diagnosis. A low result does not rule out melanoma, and a high one does not mean you have the disease.
  • Your health insurer always decides whether costs are covered.

Sun protection

  • Seek shade in strong sun
  • Wear a hat, sunglasses and clothing
  • Regularly apply sunscreen with at least SPF 30 and UVA protection to uncovered skin
  • Avoid sunbeds

Frequently asked questions

What is melanoma?

Melanoma (black skin cancer) is a malignant disease of the pigment cells of the skin. It can form secondary tumours even when small.

How do I recognise black skin cancer?

Look out for new or changed spots and check them using the ABCDE rule. Contact us promptly if a spot bleeds, weeps, itches or does not heal, or if a dark streak appears under a nail.

Does melanoma develop from a mole?

Usually not. More than two thirds of all melanomas arise on previously normal skin.

Can black skin cancer be cured?

Detected early, the outlook is very good. In the precursor stage, complete surgical removal is usually sufficient. If the cancer has spread, the outlook falls considerably.

How often should I have my skin checked?

You should look at your own skin once a month. How often a medical check-up makes sense depends on your personal risk and is decided together with you.

What is total body photography (mole mapping)?

In total body photography, also called mole mapping, the entire skin is photographed and every mole is located on a digital map. At follow-up appointments, new or changed spots become visible. The imaging complements the medical examination.

Does health insurance pay for skin cancer screening?

Basic insurance pays for screening only in the case of a family-related increased melanoma risk, and for any investigation of a specific suspicion. Otherwise, depending on the policy, supplementary insurance may cover part of it.

What does a skin check at Dermetrics cost?

The full-body skin check takes around 20 minutes and costs approx. CHF 160.
