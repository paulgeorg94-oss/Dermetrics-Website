How can I spot skin cancer early myself?

ABCDE, EFG and ugly-duckling rules, examination steps and a body map to print.

Responsible: Dr. med. Paul J. Georg, specialist in Dermatology and Venereology

In short: Look at your entire skin from head to toe once a month. Watch for the ABCDE rule, for moles that look different from all the others (the “ugly duckling”), and for raised, firm, growing lumps (EFG rule). Have new or changing spots checked by a doctor promptly. This guide does not replace a medical examination – it complements it.

Why self-examination matters

Many skin cancers are first noticed by patients themselves or by family members – often while applying cream, showering or getting dressed. Regular, attentive self-examination can help you show noticeable changes to a doctor early.

How often should I check?

Look at your entire skin from head to toe once a month, ideally on the same day each month, for example the first. Depending on your personal risk (e.g. many moles, fair skin, past sunburns, skin cancer in the family), regular full-body examinations by a dermatologist are also advisable. The intervals are determined individually according to risk.

How to examine your skin

Ideally in a well-lit room in front of a large mirror, with a hand mirror for hard-to-see areas. A second person can help check your back and scalp. Recommended order:

  1. Face, ears (also behind them), neck and scalp – part your hair with a comb or hairdryer
  2. Hands: palms, backs of the hands, between the fingers, under the fingernails
  3. Arms: from the wrists up to the shoulders, including the armpits
  4. Upper body: chest, abdomen (for women also under the breasts)
  5. Back, nape of the neck and buttocks (use a mirror or ask for help)
  6. Legs: front and back, from the thighs down to the feet
  7. Feet: soles, between the toes, under the toenails
  8. Genital area

A photo of your skin, e.g. with your smartphone, can help you recognise and compare changes over time.

The ABCDE rule: a first orientation

This rule helps, but it is not perfect: some harmless moles meet individual criteria without being dangerous. It is a first, rough assessment.

  • A – Asymmetry: One half of the spot looks different from the other.
  • B – Border: The edge is blurred, jagged or poorly defined.
  • C – Colour: Several colours in one spot (e.g. brown, black, red, white, blue).
  • D – Diameter: Larger than 6 mm (about the size of a pencil eraser).
  • E – Evolving: The spot changes in size, shape, colour or height.

Important addition: the “ugly duckling” sign

Most of a person’s moles look quite similar to each other (a “family pattern”). A mole that clearly stands out from this pattern deserves special attention – even if it does not fully meet the ABCDE criteria. Comparing moles on your own body is often more informative than assessing a single spot on its own, especially in people with many moles.

Important addition: the EFG rule

Some dangerous skin changes (so-called nodular melanomas) are single-coloured, symmetrical and smaller than 6 mm and could easily be missed with ABCDE. Three other warning signs help here, regardless of colour or symmetry:

  • E – Elevated: The spot or lump is noticeably raised above the skin surface.
  • F – Firm: It feels hard or firm to the touch.
  • G – Growing: It has grown steadily for more than a month.

Other warning signs that should always be checked

  • A wound or spot that does not heal within about four weeks
  • Itching, tenderness or bleeding without an obvious cause
  • A new reddish, scaly or crusty patch that does not go away
  • A change in existing moles, freckles or scars
  • A newly swollen lymph node area (e.g. armpit, groin, neck) if you have had skin cancer before

What to do if something looks unusual

Any new or changing spot showing one of these warning signs should be shown to a dermatologist promptly. It is better to have a harmless spot checked once too often than to miss a serious change. The final assessment – including dermoscopy – belongs in the hands of a specialist.

Don’t forget sun protection

Regular sun protection (sunscreen with an adequate sun protection factor, protective clothing, avoiding the midday sun, no sunbeds) lowers the risk of skin cancer and complements self-examination.
